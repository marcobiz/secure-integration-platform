# CRA and NIS 2 Adoption Guide for Legacy Software Vendors

This guide describes the local candidate's available controls and their limits. It is not a certification, conformity assessment or automatic compliance claim.

## Two Separate Regulatory Planes

CRA and NIS 2 should not be collapsed into one compliance claim.

The Cyber Resilience Act concerns the cybersecurity, lifecycle, vulnerability handling, technical documentation, economic-operator duties, and conformity path for a product with digital elements made available on the EU market. SIP may itself be a product with digital elements, depending on the actual distribution, manufacturer, support, packaging, commercial activity, and intended purpose. Its CRA classification remains provisional. The correct classification follows the effective function and intended purpose, not the product name.

NIS 2 concerns risk management and incident reporting duties of entities in regulated sectors. A customer subject to NIS 2 remains responsible for its own governance, risk assessment, supply-chain controls, incident handling, business continuity, access control, and notifications under the applicable national transposition. SIP can provide controls and evidence that the customer may use in its own NIS 2 programme. SIP does not make the customer, the legacy application, or the customer's organisation automatically compliant.

For Italy, customers should evaluate the official national framework, including D.Lgs. 138/2024 and the applicable ACN determinations and portal guidance for their sector, registration status, size thresholds, and designation as an essential or important entity. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 and ENISA's NIS2 technical guidance are relevant for the categories they cover, especially digital infrastructure and ICT service management providers; they should not be applied universally to every Italian customer or every legacy application without checking scope.

## SIP Intended Purpose and CRA Position

SIP runs integrations with external services on behalf of installed applications, including legacy software. The application sends data and an operation name; the Gateway authenticates the caller, checks whether that Installation is allowed to perform the operation, resolves server-side bindings, and applies external credentials on the server side. The client does not receive the vendor API key, private signing key, or private certificate material.

That intended purpose supports legacy modernization because a vendor can remove broad external credentials from distributed clients, enforce least-privilege operations through grants, revoke an Installation centrally, and keep audit metadata for security review. It does not turn SIP into endpoint protection, a universal PAM product, a SIEM, a GRC tool, a secure enclave, or a certificate authority.

The current CRA classification should remain a provisional assessment. The earlier compliance dossier identifies a prudent possible CRA position as an important product under Annex III, Class I, when SIP is marketed or used as identity/access management or privileged access management for application-to-service credentials. A default CRA product classification may also be arguable where SIP is strictly positioned as an integration gateway with internal access controls. The decision must be made on the actual distributed product, documentation, support model, commercial statements, and enabled functions.

Open-source availability and paid support do not by themselves guarantee a CRA exemption. Article 32(5) of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 provides a specific conformity-assessment path for certain free and open-source software classified as important where the technical documentation is made available to the public and the conditions of that provision are met. Whether that path is available depends on the actual product, actor role, commercial activity, documentation, and classification. It should be qualified before relying on it.

Package signing and trusted-origin controls are currently deferred by product decision. That deferral should be described as a current limitation, not as a consequence of open-source licensing. Open source lets users inspect and build code, but it does not remove the need to establish provenance, integrity, and update trust for distributed binaries, archives, images, installers, or customer-specific support packages.

## Legacy Modernization Pattern

Many legacy products embed or distribute credentials with broader authority than one customer installation needs. SIP changes that pattern by moving vendor-level external credentials to the Gateway side and limiting the client to an authenticated SIP Installation and a named operation.

For Direct clients, the application owns its SIP Installation key and calls the Gateway directly. This removes the vendor credential from the client, but the application still has local material that authenticates it to SIP. The vendor must store and rotate that local material according to the supported guidance and must treat a compromised application process as capable of invoking the operations granted to it.

For Windows Local Broker deployments, the application calls a local service through the SDK. The Broker performs local process and policy checks, protects local Installation material using Windows mechanisms such as DPAPI/CNG, and forwards authorized operations to the Gateway. The Broker does not receive vendor credentials. This keeps the Broker's Installation key out of the legacy process. The separate application-credential protection sample encrypts application-owned data at rest; when that credential is unprotected for use, plaintext returns to the application. It does not protect against Local Administrator, SYSTEM, binary replacement by a privileged actor, or malware injected into an otherwise authorized process. When plaintext application data is needed by the legacy application or returned to it, that plaintext is again inside the application's trust boundary.

The vendor must still change the legacy client enough to stop using vendor-level credentials directly, call the SIP SDK or Gateway with a narrow operation, handle denial/revocation/update states correctly, and avoid using SIP as a generic remote proxy. Business validation remains in the application and the external service. SIP grants are not a substitute for input validation, user authorisation inside the application, customer operational procedures, or endpoint security.

The audit API returns at most 1,000 records per page, with diagnostics limited to SecurityAdministrator. Follow the returned continuation as the `cursor` parameter with the same UTC bounds. There is no export button or snapshot: late events below the cursor can appear on later pages, while late events above it require an overlapping run and deduplication by event ID. Export files, destinations, access permissions and retention are customer-owned.

## Minimal Adoption Path for a NIS 2 Customer

1. Identify legacy secrets and authorities. List each vendor credential, certificate, signing key, endpoint, operation, and customer installation that the legacy product currently uses. Separate vendor-level credentials from per-installation SIP identity material.

2. Choose Direct or Local Broker deliberately. Use Direct where the application can safely own its SIP Installation key and call the Gateway. Use the Windows Local Broker where a local service boundary, process checks, and DPAPI/CNG-backed local custody help remove application-owned credentials. Do not claim the Broker defeats Administrator, SYSTEM, host compromise, or malware inside an authorized process.

3. Integrate the client narrowly. Replace direct vendor calls with SIP operations that match real business actions. Do not expose arbitrary URLs, methods, headers, credential selectors, or operation names from user input. Treat plaintext returned to the application as application data inside the application's own trust boundary.

4. Configure and publish through supported paths. Define the Connector, bind logical endpoint/secret/certificate references server-side, grant only required operations to the intended Installation, and publish with the supported validation, proposal, distinct approval, and publication workflow.

5. Test denial, revocation, and audit. Verify that ungranted operations fail before provider access, revoked Installations fail, stale or missing configuration blocks invocation, and successful and failed calls produce only the expected metadata audit. Record the exact version, commit, configuration, and test evidence used for customer acceptance.

6. Operate within the customer's NIS 2 controls. The customer remains responsible for IdP/MFA policy, RBAC assignment, backup/restore, audit retention, patch intake, incident triage, notifications, continuity planning, and periodic review. SIP already has OIDC/RBAC/four-eyes and audit foundations; this guide does not require a new SOC, SIEM, WORM archive, SSO feature, or compliance UI before adoption. Customers may integrate SIP evidence into their existing tools where their risk policy requires it.

Use the existing production OIDC integration and enforce MFA at the customer's identity provider; development identities are not production authentication. Assign and review existing tenant roles and four-eyes permissions. Choose log destination and retention under the customer's policy. Reuse the [Broker backup and recovery guidance](../user/local-broker.md) and [known limitations](../user/known-limitations.md): DPAPI blobs alone are not portable recovery material, and machine/profile loss may make protected data unrecoverable. Recovery and RTO/RPO require deployment-specific proof; no numeric objective is promised here.

## Evidence for a Distributed Version

For a version actually distributed to customers, the release owner must approve which materials are delivered. A candidate commit does not establish a release or a supported version. Review only approved and version-bound artefacts:

- source commit, tag or release identifier;
- package, image or archive name and checksum;
- SBOM for the distributed artefact where available;
- release notes and known limitations;
- security model and threat model references;
- tests and evidence applicable to that exact version and deployment path;
- vulnerability disclosure policy and support scope approved for that version;
- connector onboarding and operational guidance for the supported path.

Do not imply that a synthetic lab, preview run, or historical qualification applies to another version, another deployment model, or a customer production environment.

## What SIP Does Not Cover

SIP does not provide the customer's NIS 2 governance, management-body accountability, national registration, incident notification, risk acceptance, business continuity programme, personnel security, endpoint protection, SOC operation, or full supply-chain management.

SIP does not prove the overall security of the legacy application. The vendor still owns client-side authentication and authorisation, input validation, business logic, secure storage of any application-owned data, UI behaviour, logging policy, update mechanism, dependency management, and customer-specific deployment hardening.

SIP does not currently claim production HA/DR, production custody, universal Windows support, MSI/C ABI/COM adapter qualification, cloud qualification, production penetration testing, public certification, accreditation, or cryptographic authenticity of unsigned packages. If a package is unsigned, checksums can detect accidental or recorded changes only when the customer has an independently trusted checksum source; they do not authenticate the publisher.

## Self-Managed Boundary and ApoCert Support

The intended support model is open-source, self-managed software. ApoCert may provide professional support for integration, installation, configuration, training, troubleshooting, and hardening. NIS 2 Article 6(39) can include installation, management, operation or maintenance through assistance or active administration, on premises or remotely. Assess the actual activities, size thresholds and national transposition before excluding ApoCert from scope. That support does not silently transfer operation of the customer's Gateway, database, logs, keys, backups, availability, access reviews, or incident reporting to ApoCert.

Contracts and statements should keep that boundary explicit. ApoCert should not be described as running the customer's SOC, CSIRT, managed Gateway, managed security service, continuity process, or regulatory notification workflow unless a separate managed-service offering is deliberately created and assessed. Conversely, the phrase "support only" is not by itself a legal conclusion that ApoCert is outside NIS 2 in every scenario; actual activities, thresholds, customer contracts, national law, and ACN guidance must be checked.
